Introduction
Proportional Representation and the need for Electoral Redistricting
An electoral district, or constituency, is a geographical subdivision of a state created to provide
its population with representation in the larger state’s legislative body[(1)]. Electoral redistrict-
ing is the process of redrawing the electoral boundaries that make up constituencies. At its
most basic level, the principle motivating the necessity of redistricting is that of proportional
representation. Proportional representation (PR) is an electoral system in which the under-
lying distribution of electorate support for each political party is reflected proportionately in
the elected representative body. PR is a core feature of the Irish democratic landscape. To
accommodate PR, constituencies geographically divide the state such that the representatives
elected to each constituency are individually responsible for the same number of electorate as
any other representative in the state (although this will technically fall short on the principle
of PR for subtle reasons explained later). Between election cycles, as the population density
across the state alters, so too must the geographical boundaries of the constituencies. Election
redistricting is a necessary consequence of PR.
Electoral Redistricting in Ireland
This paper is written within the Irish context, so we must clearly define the following basic terms
for future reference:
• The national parliament of Ireland is referred to as Dáil Éireann, or simply the Dáil.
• An elected representative sitting in the Dáil is referred to as a Teachta Dála (TD).
• Ireland is divided up into 3,440 regions known as electoral divisions (EDs).
• These EDs are currently split between 39 constituencies. The current configuration of con-
stituencies in Ireland is shown in Figure 1.1. The constituency boundaries can be changed
by transferring EDs from one constituency to another. The number of constituencies is
not fixed and may be altered in a redrawing of boundaries.
• Ireland is also divided into 26 counties, which are administrative regions governed by
bodies called county councils.
The location of constituency boundaries is independent
of the position of county boundaries, but it is considered desirable for constituencies to
conform to county boundaries as far as is practicable.
On 9 February 2023, a new state body called the Electoral Commission was established
to oversee elections in Ireland. One of the roles of the Electoral Commission is reviewing the
Dáil Éireann constituencies, and making a report and recommendations in relation to possible
changes to constituency boundaries. When deciding on any changes to the current constituency
boundaries, the Electoral Commission is required to observe the following provisions of the
Constitution [2, 3]:
• Article 16.2.2°of the Constitution provides that: The number of members shall from time
to time be fixed by law, but the total number of members of Dáil Éireann shall not be fixed
at less than one member for each thirty thousand of the population, or at more than one
member for each twenty thousand of the population.
• Article 16.2.3°of the Constitution provides that: The ratio between the number of members
to be elected at any time for each constituency and the population of each constituency,
5

as ascertained at the last preceding census, shall, so far as it is practicable, be the same
throughout the country.
There is significance jointly given to other objectives which are detailed Section 57 of the
Electoral Reform Act 2022. Stated briefly, these objectives include:
1. There must be between 171 and 181 (inclusive) elected Dáil representatives.
2. Each constituency will have 3, 4, or 5 members (seats).
3. County boundaries should be preserved.
4. Constituencies must be contiguous (there cannot be isolated regions).
5. Geographic considerations to do with physical features and population density should be
considered.
6. Continuity of the current arrangement of constituencies should be upheld as much as
possible.
There is no mention of the relative importance of the themes of county boundaries, geographic
consideration and constituency continuity, though it is generally understood that PR trumps
these themes. When searching for satisfactory configurations, terms (i), (ii), and (iv) cannot
be broken, whereas the remaining (iii), (v), and (vi) can and will be transgressed in the final
boundaries to some extent.
Algorithmic Redistricting in Ireland
To the author’s knowledge, an algorithmic redistricting aid is not presently employed. Fortu-
nately, the manner of redistricting in Ireland, that of moving EDs between constituencies, lends
itself very well to a mathematical representation.
As a result, the time-intensive process of
manually generating and evaluating proposed constituencies can be dispensed with by using an
appropriate algorithm.
The main objective of this paper is to outline algorithmic techniques that can tackle the
redistricting mathematically, such that the space of possible solutions can be searched in a
unbiased and efficient manner (save for bias in the numerical-stochastic sense, though this is
a rather academic and irrelevant point for our purposes). Later in the text, we briefly touch
on further-reaching use-cases for such an algorithmic approach, namely in assessing existing
legal rules in achieving fair representation, and in testing the resilience of the rules against
gerrymandering.
Technical Problem Overview
We will refer to any one composition of Dáil constituencies and their boundaries as a “config-
uration”. From a mathematical point of view, the number of possible configurations is truly
enormous. The vast majority of the space of all possible configurations are completely unrea-
sonable from a legal perspective; a configuration selected at random will likely dramatically
fail the 171-181 seat restriction, the 3-5 seat range, the county boundary, and the contiguity
requirements simultaneously. Only a tiny sliver of the space of all possible configurations is
satisfactory according to the requirements; and even within all valid configurations, there still
exists a hierarchy from passable to optimal configurations that is impossible to rigorously probe
by a human expert.
6

Automating the task roughly splits into two main axes; mathematically quantifying the
legal rules, and, having then established a means to differentiate good from bad configurations,
efficiently navigate the possibilities in a manner which systematically finds the better solutions.
It should be noted that this process is not perfect with respect to either axis.
In the case
of codifying the rules, there is the ambiguity of legal interpretation, layered on with further
technical difficulty in faithfully translating the relative importance of the legal rules to one
another into a multi-objective mathematical summary that has the desired effect. In the case of
the search, there is the ambiguity of different configurations admitting the same score under the
chosen objective function. Both of these issues are addressed to some extent later in the text.
Very roughly speaking, and at its most basic level, the algorithms execute a search of possible
configurations as follows: Consider some initial configuration. Quantify how closely the config-
uration adheres to the rules. Make a change, e.g., take an ED , and make it part of a different
constituency. Re-evaluate how closely the changed configuration adheres to the rules. Use the
difference in the performance of the changed, and unchanged configuration to inform the search.
Continue this process iteratively until the calculated adherance to the rules is satisfactory. If
the rules have been parsed well into maths, and the search is effective, the final configuration
will be a set of constituencies that respect the Constitution and Terms of Reference.
The algorithmic solution must be formulated in a way that is interpretable and open to
scrutiny. This translates to using a minimal number of tunable parameters, such that the value
of each parameter can be subject to legal debate. A “black-box” solution, which would include
Deep Learning approaches that invoke thousands of parameters which admit highly non-linear
inter-dependencies, is deliberately avoided for this reason.

2.1
Incorporating Geographical Information
Before exploring the framing of the legal objectives as optimisable functions, we must first
consider how ED geographical information is held within the algorithm. There is no set way to
represent the information, but certainly all forms must include neighbour information, roughly
in line with Fig. [1].
The optimisation process will rely on this representation of the geography to identify which
EDs are reasonable to add to a given constituency during the search. A geographically informed
search, wherein EDs do not change their constituency membership to a random constituency,
8

but to a nearby/neighbouring constituency, is much significantly faster than a random search.
Other geographical information to do with constituency and community boundaries is overlaid
on this neighbourhood information; this is explained later.

2.3
Contiguity
The contiguity of a constituency refers to whether the constituency is whole, or split into different
regions that are not connected by land. Ignoring these exceptions momentarily for the sake
of clarity, there are standard computational techniques that can calculate whether a region
is contiguous or not stemming from graph theory. In the Irish context, contiguity is a hard
requirement. From an optimisation perspective, there is a design choice; do we constrain our
search to only configurations with contiguous regions, narrowing and hastening the search, or do
10

we entertain non-contiguous configurations during the search, in the hopes that by the time the
search completes, might it happen on a better configuration (In optimisation speak, we refer to
this as “avoiding local minima”)? If we constrain the search to only contiguous configurations,
this rules out being able to begin the search with a completely unbiased, randomised starting
point, since the constituencies would be very scattered at the start. In order to remain efficient
however, some amount of search narrowing is required. The resolution is to include a term that
severely punishes breaking contiguity, so that it is heavily disincentivised, but to also restrict
the EDs eligible to change constituency membership to those at constituency boundaries, and
to ensure that they must flip membership to that of a nearby constituency, rather than any
random one. This will have the effect of constituencies morphing, growing, and shrinking during
the search, rather than peppering the map in a non-contiguous way, while still allowing islands
to temporarily form if all other objectives deem it favourable to do so.
There are some exceptions to the land-connected contiguity definition, such as where electoral
districts which are intrinsically split. These examples are pictured in Fig. [3]. However, when
the entire ED is absorbed into the nodes as in Fig. [1], the algorithm treats these as being
land-connected, which is a fine approximation for our purposes.
2.4
Compactness
The word gerrymander comes from the combination of “Gerry”, after Elbridge Gerry, and “Sala-
mandar”, alluding to the meandering shape of a deliberately manipulated constituency. To avoid
these highly-unnaturally shaped constituencies, a compactness optimisation is performed. As
was the case with SER and variance, in order to optimise for it, we first need to measure it, and
there are a number of ways to perform this. The most intuitive, but also most computationally
demanding measure of compactness is via a calculation of the polygon enclosing the constituen-
cies, what is known as the “convex hull”. This is pictured in Fig. [4]. The computationally
demanding aspect is having to continually reference the geometry after every edition is made.
Alternatively, we may instead optimise to minimise the number of external boundary ED neigh-
bours, or maximise the number of internal boundary ED neighbours, both of which will induce
roughly circular (or at the very least non-serpentine) constituencies.
2.5
County Boundaries and Geographic Considerations
The proof-of-concept algorithms outlined in the paper have not been executed at a scale at which
the effects of optimising against county boundaries can be observed. However, the general idea
for constructing such a function is straightforward. If we consider a representation of the EDs
in the form of Fig. [1], county boundaries are the collection of graph edges connecting EDs
that are on either side of a county boundary. The most basic measure of boundary breaking
would be to count the number of times a county boundary edge connects two EDs that are
members of the same constituency. This count is then minimised during the search, minimising
the county breaks. This measure may be somewhat at odds with the practice of the Electoral
Commission, which considers a constituency that perfectly encompasses two counties to not be
breaking county borders. This discrepancy is addressed in more detail in section 5.1. There are
also certain cases where a community that is not very geographically localised may be adamantly
against being split by an electoral boundary. These may be introduced into the algorithm in
much the same way as the county boundaries; designating graph edges that will count towards a
penalty in the event that the EDs connected by the edges are members of different constituencies.

2.6
Temporal Boundaries
As outlined in the Terms of Reference, the Commission aims to maintain continuity in relation
to the existing arrangement of constituencies. Arguably, there are two ways to ensure this in
the algorithm; bias the search by using the existing boundaries as the starting configuration,
optimise for it by penalising discontinuity, or use both approaches simultaneously. To optimise
against discontinuity, we must first come up with a measure of the discontinuity. Let us introduce
a basic function fC for the purpose of illustrating this measure.
fC(Configuration) = e−acnpeople−bcnED
(8)
where npeople is the total number of people no longer in their original constituency, and
nED being the number of EDs no longer in their original constituency. The constants ac and
bc would change the relative weighting of each of these two considerations. The function fC
takes a maximum of 1 when npeople = nED = 0, so it is maximal when no change has happened,
providing an extremal value to optimise towards. However, this approach is not complete, since
it not sensitive to the underlying geography; fC might yield the same penalty for a constituency

out of equilibrium because the proportional representation optimisation function deems the 4.2
to be unacceptable, and this kind of uniqueness is preferable. The same kind of symmetries
emerge when 3 constituencies X, Y, Z with SERs of 4.33 and a bump function at 1/3, meet at
an intersection of 3 counties. While this is quite an academic point, this suggests that from an
optimisation point of view, a bump position away from 1/3 = 0.333 and 1/2 = 0.5 is preferable.
This of course must be balanced with a legal perspective on the matter, which we do not comment

2.8
Multi-Objective Optimisation
In addition to the to the difficulty of properly defining a measure for each individual constraint
and guideline (particularly county borders, geographic considerations, temporal continuity),
there are additional problems directly to do with the nature of multi-objective objective opti-
misation. There is first the legal ambiguity to do with ranking the importance of each of these
constraints relative to one another, and secondly there is the problem of faithfully translating
this hierarchy into a numerical algorithm.
The function descriptions corresponding to each of the themes in the preceding sections are
added together to give one overall optimisation function that dictates the search.
However,
the individual functions are not necessarily given the same weight in the overall optimisation
function. We refer to the individual weights assigned to the optimisation of each legal theme
the “coupling constants”. However, the technical obstacle is that there is no a priori way of
calculating exact values for these to represent a desired hierarchy of constraints.
To put it
another way, it is not as though if the coupling constant on the county boundary term is increased
+10% relative to the community boundary term that the search will then reflect this in a linear
manner with +10% more respect for the legal theme. Determining these constants thus reduces
to investigating behaviour and results of algorithms for a variety of values for each coupling
constant.

3.1
The Maths Problem
The first concept we will introduce is the term "Map". This is a collection of building blocks
that make up the region which we will be redistricting. We will refer to these building blocks as
districts. These districts, are the smallest components that can be used to build constituencies.
Constituencies, in turn, are combinations of districts, classified as either valid or invalid based
on adherence to the redistricting rule set, which defines the criteria for validity.
The challenge lies in the arrangement of districts to create valid constituencies as dictated
by the rule set. This task is complicated by the intricate interplay and ripple effects that arise
when relocating individual districts within the map. In the Irish case our map is the republic
and the building blocks are Electoral districts (EDs). The goal is to find configurations of the
EDs such that valid constituencies can be formed. The final key concept is that of "Boundaries,"
which comprises a collection of constituencies that meet the rule set criteria for a specific map.
Notably, multiple valid boundaries can exist for a given rule set, considering the variability of
seat ranges in the Irish context and or degree of acceptance for the value for the variance to
name but a few. The problem of selecting which set of boundaries to go with is covered in 3.6
So in short the problem is how does one arrange the districts (EDs) in such away as to
generate valid constituencies. Then the question is how does one sort through the final collection
of valid constituencies otherwise know as "Boundaries"? There are many steps within the overall
process from Map -> New boundary, which we will outline. The methods used within the process
are covered in 4.

3.7
Proportional Representation at the Ballot vs. Accurate Representation
during term
As stated in the outset, PR aims to reflect the political distribution of the electorate in the
makeup of the elected representatives. However, when paired with the practical need for con-
stituencies, this perhaps introduces a fundamental tension which cannot be resolved. It has
27

been acknowledged that the larger the number of seats in a constituency, the greater the oppor-
tunity for representatives of smaller parties to make it in. This points to larger constituencies
facilitating a greater resolution into the true, underlying distribution of political opinions in
the electorate, and that smaller seat constituencies coarsens the reflection of said distribution
in the representatives, favouring larger parties. However, as is demonstrable using the most
recent election data, larger constituencies increasingly enable disproportionate geographical rep-
resentation. (Matthew’s plot) Representatives can cluster geographically in one section of their
constituency. This poses an interesting, open question: If PR is supposed to facilitate a faithful,
proportionate embodiment of the constituents’ political beliefs in their representatives, do we
content ourselves with greater fidelity at the ballot at the expense of exaggerated uneven access
to representatives during the term?

Genetic Algorithm
One need only look at the world around us to appreciate the fact that evolution by natural
selection has resulted in some of the most remarkably complicated structures operating at near
optimal efficiencies in their environment. Oversimplifying the process, we can describe evolution
as a 3 step process that turns what is effectively randomly generated beings - unfit for their
environment, into their most optimal form.
1. A large group of individuals (called the population) fights amongst themselves in their
environment such that the population is "ranked" in order of "strength" or more accurately,
fitness.
2. This group of top individuals are picked out and given the highest chance of successfully
breeding.
3. Breeding causes a new generation to come about. This acts as the new population and
Step 1. is repeated.
This basis provides a good starting point to designing a computational algorithm to best use
the principles of evolution to generate optimal solutions to a problem.

Simulated Annealing
The optimisation methods for the two models presented here each rely on an objective function
to be minimised (Equation 12) or maximised (Equation 15) and an external “temperature” pa-
rameter that determines the behaviour of the corresponding algorithm. Setting this temperature
parameter at a high value results in uniform random sampling of the entire space of constituency
configurations (Figure 15a), while setting a low temperature restricts sampling around a local
extremum near the initial configuration (Figure 15b); in either case, the probability of obtaining
the desired global extremum is extremely small. If the temperature was instead initially set high
and slowly reduced over time, this would ensure that the algorithm would always be sampling
from the temperature-dependent equilibrium distribution, until it reached low temperatures and
thus the desired extremum corresponding to the most optimal configuration (Figure 15c). This
approach is called simulated annealing (10, 11), named after the technique in materials science
of heating and cooling a material in a specific way to change its atomic structure and thus its
physical properties.

6.1
Algorithmic Redistricting Framework
Benefits of Algorithmic Approach
To put it simple the problem is that of generating valid maps as defined by the rule sets that
an area writes to decide how their districts are drawn. In this paper, we employ mathematical
computational techniques to tackle the complex problem of defining valid electoral districts based
on a set of rules designed to codify aspirational goals within an electoral system. We want to
emphasise that our work is not intended to make assertions about the correctness or morality of
any specific electoral districting solution. Instead, we aim to provide a mathematical framework
that assists in the evaluation and analysis of the Irish electoral system in alignment with desired
values.
One such value we consider is the principle of equal representation, among others. We do
not prescribe a specific definition of representation, as it is inherently a normative concept, but
rather, we provide the tools necessary for individuals and policymakers to assess whether their
proposed rule sets can lead to systems that align with their values.
Our belief is grounded in the understanding that addressing normative questions effectively
often requires a deep understanding of the mathematical implications and impacts of the chosen
rules. By leveraging mathematical machinery, we offer a means for stakeholders to quantitatively
assess the consequences of their decisions and gain insights into how different rule sets may
influence outcomes.
While many nations grapple with issues related to electoral districting, Ireland faces a distinct
set of challenges. Unlike the United States, where gerrymandering has been a prominent concern,
the problem Ireland confronts is one of efficiency and ensuring that the principles of proportional
representation are upheld.
In this paper, we aim to address the specific challenges posed by electoral districting in
Ireland. Our primary objectives are twofold: to enhance the efficiency of the electoral districting
process and to explore the potential of computational methods in achieving fair representation.
We see this endeavor as an opportunity to introduce innovative techniques, previously applied
in different jurisdictions and systems, to the Irish electoral landscape.
As we delve into the intricacies of electoral districting, we aim to harness the power of
computational methods and mathematical modeling to design impartial districts, assess existing
constraints, and test the resilience of the rules against gerrymandering.
This paper does not aim to provide definitive answers but serves as a guide and a tool for
decision-makers. It offers insights into the mathematical underpinnings of electoral districting,
explores the axioms and criteria governing the process, and raises questions about the system’s
limitations. As we delve into this exploration, we hope to contribute to the ongoing discourse
on electoral fairness and representation in Ireland.
Our work hopes to show the benefits of
mathematics in shaping a more representative and equitable electoral system.
our work serves as a mathematical toolset, providing valuable assistance to those seeking
to navigate the normative complexities of electoral districting. We facilitate the exploration
of how various rules and parameters can impact the realization of desired electoral system
characteristics, without making normative judgments about what those desired characteristics
should be.
The decision support this framework provides could be critical, considering the significant
challenge the commission faces in reviewing and potentially redrawing electoral boundaries at
various levels, including EU, LEA, Dáil, and more. Especially if they all coincide together.

Heuristics for Assigning Constituencies from Past Reports
Joining two entire counties
As stated in previous Constituency Commission Reports in 2017 (7, p. 16), 2012 (6, p. 13),
2007 (5, p. 12) and (4, p. 11) the Commission has adhered to the view and practice of previous
commissions namely that joining two entire counties in a single constituency does not consti-
tute a breach of the boundary between them. This is an important precedent that we will be
using thought our submission. As we assume this view and practice will be continued by this
commission.
Balance Between The Three Constituency Sizes
As stated in previous Constituency Commission Reports in 2017 (7, p. 15) and the report in 2012
(6, p. 13) it is not a constitutional or statutory criterion that the arrangement of constituencies
should seek to achieve any particular number of, or balance between, the three constituency
sizes.
Importance of Provincial Boundaries
Within previous commissions reports and their terms of reference (4),(5),(6), and(7), there has
not been any explicit reference to the consideration of maintaining provincial boundaries.
Previous Constituency Commission reports have not explicitly stated the importance of
maintaining provincial boundaries. However, in earlier reports such as those in 2002 and 2007,
the Commission did make statements acknowledging the significance of provincial boundaries,
and that they did take them into consideration.
For example, the 2007 report stated, “Ei-
ther option would involve a breach of a county and a provincial boundary” and the 2002 report
acknowledged that the existing Longford-Roscommon constituency breached a provincial bound-
ary. In contrast, the reports from 2012 and 2017 did not mention provincial boundaries in a
similar manner.
It should be noted that previous Commissions were not required to avoid breaching provincial
boundaries. While past reports indicate that provincial boundaries have been considered in the
formation of constituencies, they have not necessarily been weighted as heavily as other factors.
For instance, in 2017, provincial boundaries were broken when an area in Offaly was added to
the Tipperary constituency. Similarly, in 2012, part of Donegal was transferred to Sligo-Leitrim,
and parts of Cavan were transferred to the same constituency. Additionally, in 2012, a portion
of Tipperary was transferred to Offaly. Thus, we believe that breaching provincial boundaries
should not preclude a potential constituency recommendation.

Variance Trade-off
The trade-off is choosing between equality of representation and respecting the terms of reference.
This trade off looks like, from a mathematical perspective, increasing the acceptable variance
up from ±5% to some higher threshold for inhomogeneity of representation to facilitate a lesser
population transfers and disruption of county boundaries. The tighter the bound on the variance
threshold, the less mathematical flexibility there is to adhere to terms of reference such as
maintaining county boundaries. In this sense, the holistic view of representation aimed at in the
terms of reference, and representation from a mathematical perspective are at odds.
An example of where this trade off might occur is with Laois and Offaly, see ?? for more
details. In short, when the two counties are grouped together they should collectively be assigned
a total of 6 seats. But at no point can the two counties independently form standalone 3 seat
constituencies within the permitted ±5%. A transfer of population and area must occur under
the mathematical purview, the question is does strictly sticking to this perspective actually
achieve greater representation? By contrast, does willingly departing from acceptable variance
in order to have the counties form standalone constituencies, adhering more closely to the terms
of reference, actually result in better representation in the holistic sense? [REFERENCE OUR
