# Learner red-team: `find_your_ed.md`

**Method.** I read only `content/module_0/find_your_ed.md`, cold, as a smart but
non-specialist Irish voter with no prior context — no ontology, no planning notes,
no sibling lessons. Going top to bottom, I logged every term used before it is
explained, every step that does not follow from what came before, every "so what?"
moment, and every interactive beat where it was unclear what I was meant to do or
what the result meant. Findings are ordered most to least confusing. I fixed
nothing.

A caveat that shapes several entries: the lesson leans on "the last lesson" for
vote weight and on the Electoral Commission as an already-known actor. In the real
course those may be established earlier. Read cold, they land as gaps, so I log
them — but flag which ones a prior lesson probably covers.

---

## 1. The "people per TD" figure is used to size EDs two sections before it is given

The lesson sizes EDs against a single TD's population well before it tells me what
that population is. At line 36 an ED is "**more people in one ED than the average
TD represents**", and at lines 59–60 the biggest ED is described as "**at nearly a
seat and a half's worth of people, that single ED strains any constituency it lands
in**". But the figure everything is measured against — 29,593 people per TD — does
not appear until the note box at line 104 and is not derived until line 141.

Reading top to bottom, at line 36 and again at line 59 I have no idea how many
people "the average TD represents", so "more than" and "a seat and a half's worth"
are comparisons to a quantity I do not yet hold. I have to take the drama on faith
and only later find out what the ruler was. This is the clearest ordering problem
in the lesson.

## 2. Constituencies elect several TDs, but I am never told why, or that Ireland uses multi-seat constituencies

The lesson repeatedly treats "a constituency elects some number of TDs" as obvious:
in the objectives, "**say which constituency it is bundled into and how many TDs
that elects**" (lines 16–17); at the reveal, "**that constituency elects a fixed
number of TDs**" (line 121); and in the real case, "**a single five-seat
constituency**" (line 181).

Cold, I do not know that Irish constituencies are multi-seat, nor what a normal
range is (three to five?). "How many TDs that elects" quietly implies the number
varies between constituencies, but nothing says so or why. A voter who pictures one
representative per area will be puzzled that a constituency has "a number" of TDs at
all. (This is plausibly established in an earlier lesson; read cold it is an
unexplained premise.)

## 3. "seat" appears as an unstated synonym for one TD

"Seat" is used as if interchangeable with a TD, but the equivalence is never stated:
"**at nearly a seat and a half's worth of people**" (line 59) and "**a single
five-seat constituency**" (line 181). At line 59 I must simultaneously work out that
a "seat" means one TD *and* recall a per-TD population I have not been given yet
(see finding 1). A reader who does not already equate seat = TD stalls here.

## 4. The Electoral Commission is introduced as an actor I am assumed to know

The body that draws the boundaries arrives fully formed: "**the unit the Electoral
Commission actually moves around when it decides which constituency you land in**"
(lines 13–15). Its function (deciding constituencies) is implied by the sentence,
which softens the gap, but I am never told what the Electoral Commission *is* — a
statutory body, when it reviews, on whose authority. It then carries most of the
lesson as "the Commission". (Likely covered in an earlier lesson; cold, it is a
named institution taken as given.)

## 5. The interactive's prompt varies, and one of its questions is hard to picture

The setup for the map explains there is no score and the prompts can be switched
off, which is good. But what it will actually ask me shifts: "**now and again it
pauses to ask you a quick question about the ED you just picked — what share of a
single TD's people it holds, how many EDs its size it would take to build the whole
constituency, or simply how many people live in it**" (lines 86–89). Reading that, I
am not sure which of the three I will be asked or whether it changes each time.

The middle option, "**how many EDs its size it would take to build the whole
constituency**", is itself a hard thing to estimate on a first encounter — it asks
me to reason about constituency size, which the lesson has not quantified at this
point. As a predict-then-reveal beat it may feel unanswerable rather than inviting.

## 6. "county-boundary breach" is dropped in as jargon

"**one of seven county-boundary breaches the Commission removed out of ten that
existed**" (lines 183–184). I can reverse-engineer from the Laois–Offaly example
that a "breach" means a constituency crossing a county line, but the term is used
before it is defined, and "seven … out of ten" invites me to picture a tally of a
thing I have only just been handed.

## 7. "population, not area" corrects a belief the lesson never planted

"**This is why population, not area, is what the map-drawing turns on**" (line 157).
Up to here nothing suggested land area might drive the map, so the correction
answers a question I had not formed and briefly reads as a non-sequitur. It is
partly rescued by the knowledge check immediately after, which surfaces the
"bigger land = more weight" misconception — but the prose asserts the contrast
before that intuition is on the table.

## 8. "TD" is never expanded

"**your ballot helps elect one set of TDs**" (line 6). For most Irish readers "TD"
needs no gloss, so this is minor — but the abbreviation is never spelled out
anywhere in the lesson, and the audience is explicitly the general electorate,
which includes people who would not name it.

## 9. "share of a TD" is used in the objectives before it is defined

The intro promises I will "**explain what its size means for your share of a TD**"
(lines 17–18). "Share of a TD" is a coined phrase that only acquires meaning in the
section "What your ED's size says about your vote" (from line 137). It is a forward
reference that resolves cleanly later, so it is low-stakes — noted for completeness.

---

## "So what?" check

The lesson is generally strong on payoff: each section ties back to the reader's
vote, and the Laois–Offaly case gives the abstract point a concrete stake. The one
place the payoff arrives before its supporting number is finding 1 — the
"seat and a half" / "more than a TD represents" beats want to feel consequential but
cannot, because the reader has not been given the yardstick yet.
